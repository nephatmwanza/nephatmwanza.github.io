I am a statistician based in Zambia, working on climate data and what it means for the people
living under it.

My training is in statistics — an MSc in Statistics on top of a BSc in Mathematics and
Statistics. My work is in climate: gridded rainfall and temperature records, extreme-event
indices, and trend analysis over southern Africa.

The combination matters more than either half. A lot of climate analysis is done by people who
know the physics but treat the statistics as a formality, or by statisticians who handle the
inference carefully but have no feel for what a number means on the ground. Sitting in both
makes it possible to ask a better question — and to know when the data cannot answer it.

## How I work

### Domain first, method second

The right index depends on the question. Zambia's rainy season can deliver a perfectly normal
seasonal total and still fail a maize crop, if the rain arrives in the wrong pattern. That is
why my recent work measures the *shape* of a season — spell lengths and percentile
exceedances — rather than its total. The statistics follow from the agronomy, not the other
way round.

### I would rather report a weak result than an impressive one

Short records with high variability often cannot support confident conclusions. Southern
Africa's rainfall swings hard with El Niño and La Niña, and forty-odd years of data is not
much against that. When the honest answer is "the signal is coherent but has not cleared
significance", that is what I write — because a programme that plans on a result which does
not replicate is worse off than one that knew the uncertainty upfront.

### I test my own analysis against known answers

Silent errors are the dangerous kind: nothing crashes, and the output looks reasonable. In my
most recent project two such bugs materially changed the results — one in a standard
statistical correction that was inflating significance, another in a widely-used climate tool
that was quietly discarding days from each season. Both were caught by running the analysis on
cases where the correct answer was known in advance. Those checks now run automatically every
time the analysis does.

### Reproducible by default

Analysis that cannot be re-run cannot be checked. My work ships as an ordered pipeline with
the environment specified and derived data included, so someone else can clone it and get my
numbers — or find where I went wrong.

## Local context is part of the method

I analyse the region I live in. When a result says dry spells are lengthening fastest in
Southern Province, I do not have to look up what that means — it is the part of the country
already most exposed to drought, and the implication for smallholder farming there is
immediate.

Development and climate-resilience organisations frequently need analysts who understand both
the statistics and the setting. That intersection is where I want to work.

## Beyond the analysis

I also teach — lecturing and tutoring in statistics and mathematics, and I hold a Diploma in
Technical and Vocational Teaching alongside my degrees. Explaining a method to someone who has
to use it is a good discipline for writing about one, and it is a large part of why I care
that findings are communicated in terms a non-specialist can act on.

I am a statistician based in Lusaka, working on climate data and what it means for the people
living under it.

My MSc in Statistics at the University of Zambia was on Zambian rainfall — specifically,
statistical projections of the mean changes in intra-seasonal rainfall characteristics over
Zambia from the reference period 1971–2000 to the future 2021–2050. That work is now under
review at *PLOS Climate*. Before and around it I worked as a research assistant on trends in temperature
and precipitation extremes over Zambia for 1981–2021, handling the climate data processing and
trend analysis; a second manuscript from that project is in preparation.

So the questions I write about here are not new to me. They are the ones I have been working
on for several years, now pursued independently and published openly.

## Statistics and climate, not one or the other

A lot of climate analysis is done by people who know the physics but treat the statistics as a
formality, or by statisticians who handle the inference carefully but have no feel for what a
number means on the ground. Sitting in both makes it possible to ask a better question — and
to know when the data cannot answer it.

## How I work

### Domain first, method second

The right index depends on the question. Zambia's rainy season can deliver a perfectly normal
seasonal total and still fail a maize crop, if the rain arrives in the wrong pattern. That is
why my work measures the *shape* of a season — onset, spell lengths, percentile exceedances —
rather than its total. The statistics follow from the agronomy, not the other way round.

### I would rather report a weak result than an impressive one

Short records with high variability often cannot support confident conclusions. Southern
Africa's rainfall swings hard with El Niño and La Niña, and forty-odd years of data is not much
against that. When the honest answer is "the signal is coherent but has not cleared
significance", that is what I write — because a programme that plans on a result which does not
replicate is worse off than one that knew the uncertainty upfront.

### I test my own analysis against known answers

Silent errors are the dangerous kind: nothing crashes, and the output looks reasonable. In my
[most recent project](/projects/zambia-rainfall-extremes/) two such bugs materially changed the
results — one in a standard statistical correction that was inflating significance, another in
a widely-used climate tool that was quietly discarding days from each season. Both were caught
by running the analysis on cases where the correct answer was known in advance. Those checks
now run automatically every time the analysis does.

### Reproducible by default

Analysis that cannot be re-run cannot be checked. My work ships as an ordered pipeline with the
environment specified and derived data included, so someone else can clone it and get my
numbers — or find where I went wrong.

## Local context is part of the method

I analyse the country I live in. When a result says dry spells are lengthening fastest in
Southern Province, I do not have to look up what that means — it is the part of Zambia already
most exposed to drought, and the implication for smallholder farming there is immediate.

Development and climate-resilience organisations frequently need analysts who understand both
the statistics and the setting. That intersection is where I want to work.

## Teaching

I have lectured mathematics and statistics for over five years across several Zambian
institutions — the University of Zambia, Evelyn Hone College, Texila American University and
Kenneth Kaunda Metropolitan University — and I hold a Diploma in Technical and Vocational
Teaching. I am a registered trained pedagogue with the Teaching Council of Zambia.

Explaining a method to someone who has to use it is good discipline for writing about one, and
it is much of why I care that findings are communicated in terms a non-specialist can act on.

## Beyond rainfall

I have also worked on climate and health, most recently as a researcher on a study assessing
the impact of climate change on child malnutrition in Zambia. Health and nutrition statistics
remain an active interest alongside climate extremes.
